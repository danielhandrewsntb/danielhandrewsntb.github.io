about/work

Me and my team mate went into this hackathon having a much better idea of what to expect, hence why we settled on an idea shortly after arrival so as to not waste precious hacking time like during the prior hackathon we partook in. We wanted to make a TTS (Text To Speech) enabled system that was connected to a device camera that would observe a chess position played over a physical board, feed it into an engine (stockfish), and play against it, calling out its moves to be executed by the user using the tts system (it was one of the categories). I began with the UI while my team mate began figuring out the logic. It should be said that we made heavy use of AI for the logic conceptualization from the get go due to our complete lack of experience using, or creating image recognition software ourselves. We did however learn from our previous experience and use a library (OpenCV), instead of trying to build everything ourselves and reinvent the wheel. I finished with the UI and moved into working on the backend with my compatriot, however this proved very slow, as we had not realized how many actual moving parts there would be when we decided on this project. We hit a roadblock with the camera detection and ended up essentially individually working on two different “forks” of the project, with his being written in javascript and mine being written in python with a dedicated camera application being used. We progressed slowly with stable frame detection and connectivity issues until we hit what would ultimately prove to be our final roadblock. It did not occur to us at the start just how much and how long the recognition software would need to be trained. And in hindsight, we should have known. Image recognition software can be trained on hundreds of thousands of images for weeks and still have a noticeable and significant level of inaccuracy. Despite realizing this around training time, we pressed on. We tried to supplement whatever images we could take (giving our images priority in the training process) with a preexisting library (despite the fact that it was a library of vector graphics, and our images were of real chess pieces, with inconsistent lighting, and angles). This process took all night, and a mishap nearly led to the entire loss of my side of the codebase. All in all, even after training on my Laptop all night (and very loudly with the pc fans desperately trying to keep my laptop from going supercritical), the software failed to recognize even the most basic of chess positions/openings. As a result, we had to scuttle the project, and were unable to present the project for judging. The training was simply too demanding (hardware, and time wise). So the takeaways from this hackathon are as follows: firstly, we should have “scouted out” the project a lot better to gauge the difficulty we’d encounter in actually building it. It should not have slipped us that it would have needed to have been trained on so much data for so long. Doing so would have allowed us to reevaluate the scope and feasibility before it was too late. A little bit of domain analysis would have gone a long way. Second, I mentioned that we split off on what was essentially two branches to try and come to a working solution (divide and conquer), and while I do maintain that it we believed it was a logical course of action at the time, I do believe perhaps we could have been more effective in our problem solving if we were troubleshooting on one version, as opposed to two. Divide and conquer is not always the best approach.
